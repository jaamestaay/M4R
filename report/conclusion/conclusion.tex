\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\section{Summary of Findings}
This thesis investigated the effects of violating conditional site independence in Bayesian phylogenetic inference and evaluated whether such data can still improve tree reconstruction accuracy. These considerations were motivated from historical linguistics, noting the main challenges of applying such methods to linguistics data.

In particular, two questions were posed as found in Section \ref{sec:qns}. The first question considered how the proportion of data that contained dependency structures affected tree reconstruction accuracy. The results obtained from Chapters \ref{sec:results} and \ref{sec:discussion} show that any misspecification of the data consistently reduced tree reconstruction accuracy, as opposed to using independently generated sites fully in the data generation method. Increasing depth of dependency had minimal additional deterioration. In fact, the main source of reduction was mainly dependent on the amount of information available within the dataset.

The second question examined if dependent data should be retained, even where its dependency structure is known. The results obtained indicate that such data can still provide considerable benefits where independent data is scarce as the marginal benefit associated with phylogenetic signal still outweigh the negative effects of model misspecification. The contrary holds in data rich scenarios, where additional signal has diminished benefit that may not justify its inclusion into the data. Similar results hold for adding sites with deeper dependency structures, although these provide less benefit than lower tier sites. 

When taken together, the results suggest that violations of conditional site independence, while introducing bias to the model, can also contribute additional information about the underlying true tree $T^*$. As such, the tradeoff between these competing effects, often times influenced by the total effective information of data, determines whether their inclusion is ultimately beneficial or detrimental to tree reconstruction and inference.

\section{Contributions and Implications}
The main contribution of this thesis is the identification of effective information as a heuristic for understanding the implications of violating conditional site independence in Bayesian phylolinguistics. The simulation results repeatedly demonstrate the influence of effective information obtained from the data, not only on reconstruction quality but in determining whether conditional sites are valuable. 

This perspective reconciles the main findings of the study, harmonising the seemingly contradictory results between Experiment 1 and 2. In Experiment 1, dependency reduces effective information contained within a fixed dataset, which thus lowers reconstruction accuracy. In Experiment 2, introducing dependency in additional data still increases effective information (albeit smaller than additional independent data) and thus improves reconstruction accuracy. As such, the problem of model misspecification on the conditional site independence assumption is largely simplified into a heuristic of considering whether the data provides adequate effective information for tree reconstruction on Bayesian phylogenetic methods. 

In the context of phylolinguistics, these results provide guidance for treatment of datasets that are suspected to have dependency structures. In highly-documented language families such as the Indo-European language family, there is no real need to include sites that violate conditional site independency as the independent signal already provides enough information. Conversely, in poorly documented language families, these sites are still valuable and should thus be retained to improve reconstruction quality, even if modelling assumptions are not met. These results were communicated to Buckley et al. (2025) who worked with loom data on Kra-dai language speakers \cite{Buckley2025}, who similarly noted that these results could support the methods used in their analysis. Additionally, the team remarked that these results could also have relevance in linguistic phylogenies, especially when dealing with morphological features that do not satisfy conditional site independence. 

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
We note several limitations of the simulation study. Firstly, the simulation study idealised several considerations related to phylolinguistic analysis. For example, rate variation (Section \ref{sec:ratevariation}) was assumed to be constant, purely binary data matrices were considered instead of matrices that may include missing data, and sites of differing tiers assumed the same parameters on each evolutionary process. The study also focussed exclusively on a singular tree prior and evolutionary process. However, real linguistic datasets exhibit more complex behaviour which are not reflected in this study. Consequently, the quantitative results presented should not be interpreted as universal estimates of the effects of violating the conditional site independence assumption. Future work could extend the ideas portrayed in this study by implementing relevant complexities that better represent linguistic data, perhaps implementing such models to datasets of languages with known phylogenies. These implementations would help assess the practical relevance of the observed tradeoffs and how these findings generalise beyond simulated settings.

Next, the study focussed solely on the consequences of dependency using MCMC which assumes conditional site independence. Alternative modelling approaches that allow for dependency structures within sites were not considered. Future work could look into whether explicitly accounting for dependency indeed improves tree reconstruction quality, as opposed to the heuristics suggested in this thesis.

Finally, effective information was presented primarily as an interpretative framework, rather than a formally defined quantity. Developing a rigorous theoretical measure of effective information under different dependency structures, perhaps by considering entropy and other results in information theory, would provide a stronger mathematical foundation for the empirical findings presented. Such a framework may allow for quantitative decision-making on including dependent sites for inference.

Ultimately, the thesis shows that the consequences of violating conditional site independence on reconstruction quality are more nuanced than simply removing invalid data. While these dependencies within the data introduce bias via incorrect likelihood calculations, they may still provide valuable phylogenetic information. As such, the effectiveness of including such data depends on the balance between these competing forces. As unknown dependencies between sites are often unavoidable in historical linguistics, understanding the trade-offs is crucial for tree reconstruction, which in turn give us insights into the history of language evolution.
