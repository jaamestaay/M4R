\section{Experiment 1 - Impact of Dependence on Reconstruction Accuracy}
\label{sec:discussionexp1}
From the findings of Experiment 1, we answer the effects of applying Bayesian phylogenetic analysis on misspecified data - that is, data that does not satisfy conditional site independence. The principal result as detailed through Section \ref{sec:exp1results} is that any violation of the independence assumption hurts tree reconstruction accuracy, but the amount of dependence matters less. The drop in performance from data fully satisfying the assumption to any amount of dependent data is ubiquitous across Figures \ref{fig:exp1nrfmainsmall}, \ref{fig:exp1clademainsmall}, \ref{fig:exp1nrfcons}, \ref{fig:exp1cladecons}, \ref{fig:exp1nrfmcc} and \ref{fig:exp1clademcc}. This represents the primary fault of model misspecification as detailed in Section \ref{sec:misspecification}: by assuming conditional site independence, the likelihood evaluations assume that the data provides more (effective) information about true tree $T^*$, whereas the dependency structure decreases the effective information gained from all dependent samples. 

However, it is notable that varying the depth of dependency has little effect on tree reconstruction. The relative invariance across depth suggests that, within the parameter ranges considered, dependency depth has substantially less impact on reconstruction quality than the initial violation of independence. This can be explained by the fact that all dependencies are derived from a fixed proportion of the data set which is independently generated - thus all dependencies encode similar information that contributes to the independent site they inherit from. We can then note that the relevant quantity for reconstruction is not the total number of sites $S$ but rather the total number of independent sites $N_1$. This is seen through the differences in tree reconstruction quality between varying proportions of independent data, albeit having a smaller impact than the initial violation of conditional site independence.

We note that across all dependency configurations, the main improvement in tree reconstruction quality is due to increasing $S$. For example, reconstructing true tree $T^*$ with $n$ languages will always perform better with $5000$ sites of data than $500$, even if the data violates conditional site independence. This means dependent sites do carry phylogenetic signal that, while less informative than independent sites, still contributes meaningfully to likelihood calculations and outweighs the losses from model misspecification in high data regimes. At large $S$, the posterior is already tightly concentrated around the true tree, and thus misspecification is practically irrelevant. On the contrary, the issues posed by model misspecification are most obvious in scenarios where only a small amount of data for this language exists. This extends the conclusion found in Section \ref{sec:sizevalidation}: increasing data quantities has a larger effect that preserving independence within the parameter space considered.

Finally, we considered the differences in tree reconstruction for MRC and MCC trees. MRC trees tend to overperform with regards to nRF as the tree absorbs uncertainties into multifurcations rather than making clear choices. A possible explanation is that, under model misspecification, the posterior may be overconfident through treating correlated sites as independent ones, which MRC is able to account for by practicing conservative clustering. On the other hand, MCC trees outperform MRC trees with regards to clade recovery since the MCC trees prioritise posterior probability which allows the model to retain true clades more often, even under misspecification. As such, the choice of summary tree should therefore be determined based on priorities, choosing MRC for overall tree reconstruction accuracy, while choosing MCC when clades and groups of languages are of interest.

\section{Experiment 2 - Efficacy of Additional Dependent Data}
\label{sec:discussionexp2}
The findings of Experiment 2 highlight the efficacy of including known dependent data into Bayesian phylogenetic analysis. Additional information, albeit violating the conditional site independence assumption of the model, is most beneficial where independent size $N_1$ is small and the tree space is large. These effects are clearly seen across Figures \ref{fig:exp2nrfmainsmall}, \ref{fig:exp2clademainsmall}, \ref{fig:exp2nrfcons}, \ref{fig:exp2cladecons}, \ref{fig:exp2nrfmcc} and \ref{fig:exp2clademcc}, where both nRF and clade recovery improves in subplots nearer the bottom left of the figures. These gains can be substantial, providing up to a 27.6\% increase in recovering true clades (when the independent data only reproduced about 25\% in the first place), regardless of dependency scheme. As such, we can conclude that where the model is \textit{starved} of information, a set of correlated sites still provides valuable information, albeit less so than truly independent sites. This information is able to outweigh the penalties from overestimating total information and thus be beneficial to analysis.

However, we note that as $N_1$ increases, the independent data alone produce a high level of tree reconstruction quality. This means that the marginal benefit by adding dependent data is much smaller than in the data sparse case. This means that the misspecification introduced by the dependent data introduces noise into the posterior by inflating total information gained from the dataset. We see that in the aforementioned figures, subplots nearer the top right either see much weaker improvements than in data sparse situations or even deprovements in tree reconstruction. Crucially, this has implications on decisions made on including sites that violate conditional site independence. These results suggest that dependent data is most valuable where independent data alone cannot provide a strong enough signal for tree reconstruction.

These effects also vary with respect to dense and sparse dependency structures, identified through the associated ZIP random variable with parameters $p$ and $\lambda$. We note that denser methods, which produce dependencies on sites more evenly, consistently produces better tree reconstruction quality than sparse methods. As noted in Section \ref{sec:exp2results}, this difference in tree reconstruction quality suggests that larger clusters of sites dependent on a single site will contribute less effective information about the tree than the same number of sites that are instead dependent on multiple sites. Put another way, increasing the total number of lineages present in the dependent sites leads to more effective information gained from dependent sites, increasing the benefits associated with appending such sites to the model. The contrary is true in sparser schemes, which also lead to higher variance amongst the posterior and increasing uncertainty in estimating $T^*$. This thus has implications not only on whether or not dependent sites are retained for phylolinguistic analysis, but also on the dependency structure within the sites themselves. 

Finally, we discuss the diminishing returns by including each additional tier of sites into the analysed data. As we increase the tiers, the construction method defined in Section \ref{sec:zip} suggests that the total number of $0$s in each site increases. This reduces the ability of sites generated in deeper tiers to vary much from their parent sites, which reduces the amount of effective information gained from including them. These effects are most clearly seen in the diminishing benefits from including the fourth tier of sites, providing negligible reconstruction benefit while continue to inflate the sample size assumed by the model.

\section{Impact of Effective Information on Tree Reconstruction}
While the findings conclude that data that violates conditional site independence has a small yet noticeable negative effect on tree reconstruction (as opposed to independently generated data), we note that these effects vary across different scenarios. There seems to be an underlying factor that is key to tree reconstruction quality in Bayesian phylolinguistics: the effective information gained from the data at hand. This introduces a tradeoff between the bias caused by model misspecification, and the accuracy gained by introducing additional information into the model. This gives reason to why any negative effects of model misspecification on a fixed data set are magnified in low data scenarios, and why the positive effects of adding dependent data diminish as more independent sites are introduced.

This corroborates with the theoretical analysis derived in Chapter \ref{sec:background}. With independent sites, we face no issues of model misspecification, and additional information is gained additively as found in (\ref{eq:independentbreakdown}). However, as found in (\ref{eq:dependentlikelihood}), where dependent sites are concerned, the model introduces bias via incorrect likelihood calculations, and the amount of effective information gained is stunted by their dependency structures. These results are portrayed in the tradeoffs found in Experiments: where the benefits of effective information gained by correlated sites compete with the losses of bias induced by model misspecification. This relationship can also be seen in the type of dependency structure found in Experiment 2, where tiers of dependent sites generated by denser methods provide more effective information than tiers of sites generated by more sparse methods.

\section{Implications for Phylolinguistics}
As such, we can conclude that while violations of the conditional site independence assumption in Bayesian phylogenetic analysis do produce measurable bias in tree reconstruction, the effects of these violations are conditioned on the amount of information present in the data itself. Experiment 1's findings suggest that in low information settings, any presence of dependence data within the data matrix $\mathbf{D}$ causes sizable deprecation of tree reconstruction quality, while these effects are much more trivial in high information settings (that is, where the total number of sites $S$ is already high). Experiment 2's findings extend these conclusions to provide evidence that in low (independent) information settings, the inclusion of data that is known to violate conditional site independence has much benefit in tree reconstruction, but is less useful or even harmful in high information settings. 

These findings thus form a heuristic framework for analysing the efficacy of dependent data in Bayesian phylolinguistic analysis, which are heavily dependent on the amount of independent data already available in the dataset. This not only sets the expectation for the losses of model misspecification but also the amount of marginal benefit gained from including additional sites that violate conditional site independence. Additionally, when considering additional dependent sites, it is worth considering the likely dependency structure present within the data - dense structures would lead to larger improvements in tree reconstruction than sparse structures.

We consider the application of these results in the context of historical linguistics. Häuser et al. (2026) notes the current limitations of importing Bayesian phylogenetic analysis into historical linguistics - one of which being that high-quality cognate datasets are scarce and small and thus are unsatisfactory for phylogenetic analysis \cite{Hauser2026}. While this is an issue already being resolved through the generation of larger, standardised datasets for language families like the Indo-European langauge family \cite{Anderson2025}, lack of data plagues less-documented language families in tree reconstruction. The results of the simulation study in this thesis can thus be applied in these respective scenarios, especially where there exist sites based on non-language cultural factors for language families to incorporate.

One such application of these results can be found in incorporating loom data into phylolinguistic analysis, as performed by Buckley et al. (2025) \cite{Buckley2025}. The paper compared the tree reconstruction ability of the Bayesian phylogenetic model on (1) the language data set, which comprised $653$ sites, against that of (2) the loom data set, which comprised 216 sites which had a known dependency structure, similar to what has been described in Section \ref{sec:dependent}, with only 36 independent sites. The data was collated across 29 language-loom pairs and the consensus trees of both data sets were compared. In the case of the language data set, the findings of Experiment 1 suggest that the potential violations of conditional site independence are not significant to tree reconstruction, although some divergences of clades can be explained via model misspecification. In the case of the loom data set, the inclusion of higher tiers (or levels) of data may be justified from the results obtained in this thesis: having only 36 independent sites suggests that correlated data is still able to improve clade recovery and tree reconstruction.
