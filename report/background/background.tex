\chapter{Background}
\label{sec:background}
In Chapter \ref{sec:introduction}, we established that Bayesian phylogenetic methods are the principal method for recovering language evolution in historical linguistics. However, the method hinges on the critical assumption of site independence. Before investigating how violations of site independence affect inference, we focus on understanding the technical framework underpinning these methods. 

This chapter provides the mathematical foundation for the simulation study found in later chapters. We start by building on the definitions found in Chapter \ref{sec:introduction} to define the phylogenetic trees and evolutionary models that describe linguistic evolution in Section \ref{sec:phylogenetictrees}, while also describing the Bayesian inference methods on the space of phylogenetic trees $\mathcal{T}$, including the computational implementation on the evolutionary analysis program BEAST. We discuss the implications of imposing site independence on data in Section \ref{sec:independent}, before turning to the dependent data generation methods and thus implications on relaxing the site independence assumption on data in Section \ref{sec:dependent} and discuss the possible impacts of model misspecification. Finally, we analyse both tree recovery metrics and algorithm diagnostics to quantify accuracy and ensure quality of simulation in Section \ref{sec:evalmetrics}. 

\section{Bayesian Phylogenetic Inference}
\label{sec:phylogenetictrees}
We start by considering the space of language (or in this case, phylogenetic) trees $\mathcal{T}$, building on the earlier definitions found in Chapter \ref{sec:introduction}. In particular, we focus our discussion on the space of bifurcating trees that have branches with lengths proportional to time, and assume that lexical data $\mathbf{D}$ can be generated via evolutionary models with parameteres $\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \in \boldsymbol{\Theta}$ on a true tree $T^* \in \mathcal{T}$.

When considering phylogenetic trees in a Bayesian setting, 
a natural starting point is to consider Bayes' theorem in the given context:

\begin{definition} [Bayes' theorem]
    For a given data set $\mathbf{D} \in \{0, 1\}^{n \times S}$ and model parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \boldsymbol{\Theta}$, we consider a tree $T \in \mathcal{T}$. Bayes' theorem states that the probability of the tree given the data and evolutionary model (the \textbf{posterior} probability) is proportional to the probability of observing the data given the tree and the evolutionary model (the \textbf{likelihood}) multiplied by the joint probability of the tree and evolutionary model (the joint \textbf{priors}):
    \begin{align}
        P(T | \mathbf{D}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) &= \frac{P(\mathbf{D} | T, \boldsymbol{\theta})P(T, \boldsymbol{\theta})}{P(\mathbf{D})} \\
        &\propto P(\mathbf{D} | T, \boldsymbol{\theta})P(T,\boldsymbol{\theta}) \label{eq:bayestheorem}
    \end{align}
\end{definition}

We note that (\ref{eq:bayestheorem}) follows naturally since we can treat $P(\mathbf{D})$ as a (unknown) constant. Additionally, consider $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ as the set of all model parameters from the evolutionary model and the tree prior. Our goal is to model the distribution of trees informed by the data and the parameters, or the posterior distribution $P(T | \mathbf{D}, \boldsymbol{\theta})$, either by direct calculation or by generating samples that follow it. However, due to the intractability of $P(\mathbf{D})$, it is unfeasible to explore the former method. We are therefore required to generate samples (of trees in space $\mathcal{T}$) that take on the posterior distribution. In many Bayesian methods, this requires defining and calculating likelihood $P(\mathbf{D} | T, \boldsymbol{\theta})$ and priors $P(T, \boldsymbol{\theta})$.

\subsection{Tree Priors: Yule models}
We first consider defining the prior of the model, by decomposing it further as
\begin{equation}
    P(T, \boldsymbol{\theta}) = P(T|\boldsymbol{\theta})P(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \label{eq:priors}
\end{equation}
where $P(T|\boldsymbol{\theta})$ represents the tree prior, while $P(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ considers the (joint) distribution of all parameters of the model. While there exists many methods of generating trees, there are only a select few which are applicable in the context of historical linguistics \cite{hoffmann_bayesian_2021}. We limit our discussion to an elaboration of a the Yule model, common tree prior used in Bayesian phylogenetic model.

The Yule model \cite{Yule1925} can be defined as a pure birth process (see Definition \ref{def:birthprocess}) with a single intensity $\lambda \geq 0$, where $N_t$ refers to the number of branches at time $t$, $N_0 = 1$. We make a distinction here that the model is generated from the ancestral lineage and thus encodes time $0$ as the oldest point of the analysis, while final time $T$ where there are $n$ tips for $n$ languages represents the present. This is contrary to the common representation of time in phylogenetic analysis, where time $0$ represents the present age, or agglomerative tree generation methods like the coalescent process. Conditioning on the tree having $n$ tips and root age $T$, the prior distribution takes on form \cite{Gernhard2008}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:yuleprior}
    P(T | \lambda, n) = n! \lambda^{n-1}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\exp(-\lambda T)
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the only relevant parameter in $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ that affects the tree prior.

\begin{definition} [Birth Process \cite{Durrett2016}]
\label{def:birthprocess}
A birth process with intensity $\lambda \geq 0$ is a continuous-time stochastic process $\{N_t\}_{t \geq 0}$ with values in $\mathbb{N}_0$ that satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $N_t$ is a non-decreasing process: $N_0 \geq 0$ and $s < t \implies N_s \leq N_t$,
    \item The infinitesimal transition probabilities model single arrival: for $t \geq 0, \delta > 0, n, m \in \mathcal{N}_0$,
    \begin{equation}
        P(N_{t + \delta} = n + m | N_t = n) = \begin{cases}
            1 - \lambda \delta + o(\delta) & m = 0 \\
            \lambda \delta + o(\delta) & m = 1 \\
            o(\delta) & m > 1,
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    \item $N_t$ has conditionally independent increments: $\forall s < t$, $N_t - N_s$ is independent of all arrivals prior to $s$ conditional on the value of $N_s$.
\end{enumerate}
where function $f$ is $o(\delta)$ if $\lim\limits_{\delta \to 0^+} \frac{f(\delta)}{\delta} = 0$.
\end{definition}

This model, as with all tree priors, incorporates the linguistic assumption that language diversify through repeated divergence events without horizontal transfer. As such, this adds a slight complexity to data generation: words or lexical items that are borrowed must be removed, which may require further linguistic arguments to determine \cite{Campbell2004}. We also note that due to the single arrival characteristic of Yule model, only bifurcating trees are generated. Moreover, since the model does not allow for deaths to occur, the model generates rooted binary trees whose tips are all extant languages. This means that all trees generated by the Yule model are \textit{ultrametric}, where the distance from (or age of) the root from every language is identical. This presents an additional assumption of employing such a method: all languages are assumed to be extant at the present age. While this reduces the complexity of the model and reduces the number of parameters introduced by the tree prior (and thus simplifying (\ref{eq:yuleprior})), this means that any dataset that includes extinct languages in its dataset is misspecified by such a model and may introduce bias, especially with respect to rates of evolution and node ages \cite{hoffmann_bayesian_2021}. As such, other tree priors like birth-death processes would be of interest \cite{Gernhard2008}. For the purposes of this simulation study, we will assume that all languages are extant.

\subsection{Evolution Model: Continuous-Time Markov Models}
\label{sec:ctmc}
Having defined the tree prior, evolutionary models attempt to explain the presence or absence of sites across the languages of the model. Since the data is binary, we need only consider two types of states: the presence (1) or absence (0) of a site. As the model is defined on continuous time, the binary continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) model is a natural method to define the evolutionary process.

The process assumes the Markov property (see Definition \ref{def:markov}), which can be clearly justified in the context of historical linguistics. {\color{red} maybe justify this.} This induces the infinitesimal rate (or \textit{generator}) matrix $\mathbf{G} := (g_{ij})$ (see Definition \ref{def:generator}), which results in a time-dependent transition probability matrix to be the entries of the matrix \cite{Anderson1991}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transitionmatrixevolution}
    \mathbf{P}(t) = \exp(\mathbf{G}t).
\end{equation}

\begin{definition} [Markov Property \cite{Durrett2016}]
\label{def:markov}
    A continuous-time process $\{X_t\}_{t \geq 0}$ defined on state space $\mathcal{S} = \{0, 1\}$ satisfies the Markov property if 
    \begin{equation}
        P\left(X_{t_n} = i_n | X_{t_{n-1}} = i_{n-1}, \dots, X_{t_1} = i_{n-1}\right) = P\left(X_{t_n} = i_n | X_{t_{n-1}} = i_{n-1}\right),
    \end{equation}
    for all $i_1, \dots, i_n \in E, \ 0 \leq t_1 < \cdots < t_n < \infty$, where $n \in \mathbb{N}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition} [Generator \cite{Durrett2016}]
\label{def:generator}
    The generator $\mathbf{G} := (g_{ij})_{i, j \in E}$ of the family of transition probability matrices $\{\mathbf{P}_t | t\geq 0 \}$ is given as 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{G} := \lim\limits_{\delta \to 0^+} \frac{1}{\delta}(\mathbf{P}_t - \mathbf{I}),
    \end{equation}
    where $g_{ij}$ represents the exponential rate of moving from state $i$ to state $j$ where $i \neq j$, and $q_{ii} = -\sum_{j\neq i} q_{ij}$ are the outgoing rates.
\end{definition}

The computation of these probabilities are key to calculating the likelihood $P(\mathbf{D} | T, \boldsymbol{\theta})$. To facilitate efficient computation, we assume the existence of a unique time-reversible stationary distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi} = (\pi_0 \ \pi_1)$, where 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:detailedbalance}
    \boldsymbol{\pi}\mathbf{G} = \mathbf{0}, \ \  \pi_0 (\mathbf{P}(t))_{01} = \pi_1 (\mathbf{P}(t))_{10} \ \forall t.
\end{equation}

While there are many other evolutionary models, we base our exploration in the binary CTMC model. The evolutionary model used in Gray and Atkinson's seminal paper \cite{Gray2003}, the model assumes that the data $\mathbf{D}$ follows the transition diagram described in Figure \ref{fig:markovchain}. This implies exactly two parameters for the evolutionary model, given by $\{q_{01}, q_{10}\}$, where 
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{G} &= \begin{pmatrix}
        - & q_{01} \\ q_{10} & -
    \end{pmatrix} = \beta\begin{pmatrix}
    - & \pi_1 \\ \pi_0 & -
    \end{pmatrix}, \label{eq:parameters} \\
    \boldsymbol{\pi} &= (\pi_0, \pi_1) = \left(\frac{q_{10}}{q_{01}+q_{10}}, \frac{q_{01}}{q_{01}+q_{10}}\right),
\end{align}
where the $-$ are the conventional notation used implied diagonal entries as defined in Definition \ref{def:generator} and $\beta = {q_{01} + q_{10}}$ is a scalar constant \footnote{Equation \ref{eq:parameters}, that is, that the stationary distribution of a binary CTMC is proportional to the transition rates of the chain, is proven in Appendix \ref{app:stationaryprob}.}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{report/figures/ctmc.png}
    \caption{Transition Diagram of binary CTMC model.}
    \label{fig:markovchain}
\end{figure}

We are thus able to calculate the likelihood $P(\mathbf{D}|T, \boldsymbol{\theta})$ for the model. For the sake of demonstration, we illustrate the likelihood only for a single site $\mathbf{D}_i$, saving full derivations for $\mathbf{D}$ in Sections \ref{sec:independent} and \ref{sec:dependent}. Given the binary phylogenetic tree $T = (V, E, r)$ with $n$ observed languages, where sites evolve via a binary CTMC with generator $\mathbf{G}$ and stationary distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi}$, the likelihood for a site $\mathbf{D}_i$ is

\begin{align}
    P(\mathbf{D}_i | T, \mathbf{G}) &= \sum_{\mathbf{x}_W \in \{0, 1\}^{|W|}} P(\mathbf{D}_i, \mathbf{x}_W | T, \mathbf{G}, \boldsymbol{\pi}) \\
    &= \sum_{\mathbf{x}_W \in \{0, 1\}^{|W|}} P(X_r = x_r) P(\mathbf{D}_i, \mathbf{x}_{W \setminus \{r\}} | T, \mathbf{G}, X_r = x_r) \\
    &= \sum_{\mathbf{x}_W \in \{0, 1\}^{|W|}} P(X_r = x_r) \prod_{(u, v) \in E} P(X_v = x_v | X_u = x_u), 
\end{align}

where $X_u$ is the random variable of the state at node $u$, and we employed the Markov property of the CTMC model. As defined previously, the first term corresponds to the stationary distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ This reduces the problem to calculating transition probabilities, where (\ref{eq:transitionmatrixevolution}) is most relevant. This gives the expression

\begin{align}
    P(\mathbf{D}_i | T, \mathbf{G}) &= \sum_{\mathbf{x}_W \in \{0, 1\}^{|W|}} \pi_{x_r} \prod_{(u, v) \in E} \mathbf{P}(t_{u\to v})_{x_u x_v} \\
    &= \sum_{\mathbf{x}_W \in \{0, 1\}^{|W|}} \pi_{x_r} \prod_{(u, v) \in E} \exp(\mathbf{G}t_{u\to v})_{x_u x_v} \label{eq:sitelikelihood}
\end{align}

This decomposition is inefficient for large values of $n$: the set of internal nodes $W$ grows linearly with $n$, thus exponentially increases the number of internal terms in the sum of (\ref{eq:sitelikelihood}). Felsenstein (1973) introduced an efficient ``pruning'' method {\cite{Felsenstein1973}}, exploiting the structure of the tree to recursively evaluate likelihoods of subtrees \cite{Felsenstein2004}. This allows for efficient likelihood calculation that grows linearly rather than exponentially with $n$.

For a simulation study, the generation of data through such evolutionary processes require particular consideration. For a given tree $T$, at each site, the state at the root is sampled and propagated down the tree via the CTMC model along each branch that has transition probabilities $\mathbf{P}(t)$ via (\ref{eq:transitionmatrixevolution}). This results in the generation of the site $\mathbf{D}_i$. If the assumption of conditional site independence is held, the process is repeated for every single site until the full binary data matrix $\mathbf{D} \in \{0, 1\}^{n \times S}$ is generated. This generative process is defined in Algorithm \ref{algo:ctmcdatageneration}.

We note an additional constraint in the context of historical linguistics: sites are excluded if they are absent in all languages considered. This means that if certain sites are generated as a zero matrix $\mathbf{0}$, a process of \textit{ascertainment} takes place, where these sites are removed \cite{hoffmann_bayesian_2021}. This decreases the number of sites that are generated within the data. In order to account for this practically, an inflated value of $S$ is set, before performing sampling without replacement to derive the dataset $\mathbf{D}^{n\times S}$.

The binary CTMC model is highly favoured since it is highly parsimonious and is computationally tractable. The evolution model introduces few parameters as discussed above, making likelihood calculations and thus Bayesian phylogenetic inference more efficient. However, these constraints insert more assumptions about the homogeneity and memorylessness properties of transitions between only two states, which may be an oversimplification of the complexity of evolution in real languages. Studies have shown that more complex models such as the binary covarion model \cite{Tuffley1998} perform better on linguistic data through allowing slow and fast rates of change \cite{hoffmann_bayesian_2021}. 

For the context of the simulation study conducted in this thesis, we limit our discussion and implementation to the binary CTMC. This provides a powerful baseline model to study the effects of model misspecification, where more complex evolutionary models can be discussed in future work.

\subsection{Rate Variation}
\label{sec:ratevariation}
As noted by Bergsland and Vogt (1962), the rates of evolution across lineages and subsets of data varies considerably \cite{Bergsland1962}. For example, sites belonging to a meaning with few cognates have been shown to empirically evolve more slowly than a meaning with many cognates \cite{Pagel2006}. In order to accommodate for this, a site-specific rate multiplier $r_i$ is included in (\ref{eq:transitionmatrixevolution}). This includes $S$ more parameters for model, where the method of generating these rates (and thus their priors) is most commonly a Gamma distribution with mean $1$ and shape parameter $\alpha$ \cite{hoffmann_bayesian_2021}. Such a method preserves the expected rates, while allowing for substantial variation across sites. In practice, this improves the model by measuring heterogeneity of site evolution, which may support fitting real language data. 

For the sake of the simulation study contained within this thesis, rate variation is intentionally excluded - all sites are assumed to evolve with a fixed rate $r_i = 1$. This is a design choice, with an objective to isolate the effects of model misspecification through conditional site independence. Allowing the rate variation would introduce additional structure that could confound inference, making it difficult to attribute observed effects as due to the violation of conditional site independence. This reduces the model to a \textit{homogeneous} CTMC model instead.

\subsection{Posterior Sampling with Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods}
\label{sec:mcmc}
Having discussed the prior and likelihood probabilities, we are now well equipped to discuss sampling from the posterior.  As mentioned prior, the normalising constant of the posterior is often difficult to compute, which makes analytic expressions of the posterior distribution inaccessible. However, there are additional considerations in the context of historical linguistics, even if one were to apply a sampling based method. The size of the space of all possible binary phylogenetic trees $\mathcal{T}$ grows \textit{super}-exponentially (that is, grows at a rate faster than exponential increase) with the number of languages $n$ \cite{Schroeder1870}. In fact, the problem of finding the optimal tree is \textit{NP-hard} \cite{Foulds1982}. Moreover, the priors are defined on both discrete and continuous distributions, complicating the analysis of the model.

These challenges motivate the use of simulation-based approaches to approximate the posterior distribution. Bayesian phylogenetic inference commonly relies on Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods to generate samples from $p^\star := P(T | \mathbf{D}, \boldsymbol{\theta})$ without the explicit computation of the normalising constant of ($\ref{eq:bayestheorem}$). The method attempts to construct a Markov chain of trees (and parameters) whose stationary distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ is equal to $p^\star$, such that states of the chain are regarded as (dependent) samples from the posterior distribution. More formally, suppose we initialise the chain at some $\mathbf{z}^{(0)} = \{T^{(0)}, \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}\}$, we generate chain $\{\mathbf{z}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{z}^{(t)}, \dots\}$ such that $T^{(t)} \sim p^\star$ after the chain is allowed to mix for an amount of time called the \texttt{burnin}. 

This subsection aims to explain the methodology used in MCMC. In practice, these considerations within the algorithm have been neatly summarised in packages and applications such as BEAST, which will be discussed in Section \ref{sec:beast}.

As the name of the algorithm suggests, the model generates a chain which fulfills the Markov property. This means that, given a current sample $\mathbf{z}^{(n)}$, in order to determine the next sample $\mathbf{z}^{(n+1)}$, we propose some $\mathbf{z}' \sim q(\cdot | \mathbf{z}^{(n)})$ by some \textit{proposal distribution} $q$, before assessing whether the proposal is suitable. 

This proposal distribution $q$ is clearly multivariate in the context of historical linguistics, generating a new tree $T \in \mathcal{T}$ but also other parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \boldsymbol{\Theta}$. We assume that $q$ can be decomposed into independent proposals, particularly of $T$, and that all other parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ can be proposed in a straightforward manner via conventional probability distributions. The proposal of a new tree $T'$ given tree $T^{(n)}$ is clearly non-trivial due to its non-Euclidean nature and requires extra treatment. There are many methods, such as the Nearest Neighbour Interchange (NNI) \cite{Li1996}, the Subtree Prune and Regraft (SPR) \cite{Hillis1996} and time-scaling operations, that generate a stochastic walk over the tree space $\mathcal{T}$. 

Once the proposal $\mathbf{z}' = \{ T', \boldsymbol{\theta}' \}$ has been chosen via the proposal distribution $q$, it is evaluated based on an acceptance probability:

\begin{align}
    \alpha(\mathbf{z}^{(n)}, \mathbf{z}') &= \min \Bigg\{1, \frac{p^\star(\mathbf{z}')q(\mathbf{z}^{(n)}|\mathbf{z}')}{p^\star(\mathbf{z}^{(n)})q(\mathbf{z}'|\mathbf{z}^{(n)})}\Bigg\} \\
    &= \min \Bigg\{1, \frac{P(\mathbf{D} | \mathbf{z}')P(\mathbf{z}')q(\mathbf{z}^{(n)}|\mathbf{z}')}{P(\mathbf{D} | \mathbf{z}^{(n)}))P(\mathbf{z}^{(n)}))q(\mathbf{z}'|\mathbf{z}^{(n)})}\Bigg\},\label{eq:mhacceptance}
\end{align}

that determines whether we accept the proposal as the next sample or not:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{z}^{(n+1)} = \begin{cases}
        \mathbf{z}' & \text{with probability } \alpha(\mathbf{z}^{(n)}, \mathbf{z}') \\
        \mathbf{z}^{(n)} & \text{with probability } 1 - \alpha(\mathbf{z}^{(n)}, \mathbf{z}').
    \end{cases}
\end{align}

Equation \ref{eq:mhacceptance} is paramount to the MCMC algorithm, state in full in Algorithm \ref{algo:mh}. The acceptance ratio is designed such that the resulting Markov chain $\{\mathbf{z}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{z}^{(t)}, \dots\}$ has stationary distribution $p^\star$\cite{Douc2019}. Crucially, the method does not require the knowledge of marginal probability $P(\mathbf{D})$, which allows the acceptance probabilities to be easily calculated by decomposition of the posterior probabilities into the likelihood and prior probabilities. 

% MCMC Algorithm
\begin{algorithm}
\SetAlgoLined
\SetKwInOut{KwInput}{Input}
\SetKwInOut{KwOutput}{Output}
\SetKw{Return}{return}
\SetKwProg{Fn}{Function}{}{end}
\LinesNumbered
\KwInput{
    $N$, number of samples to be generated, \\
    $\mathbf{z}^{(0)}$, starting point of Markov chain.
}

\BlankLine
\KwOutput{$\mathbf{Z} = \{\mathbf{z}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{z}^{(N)}\}$, resulting Markov chain with stationary distribution $p^\star$.}
\BlankLine
\For{$n = 1, \dots, N$}{
    Sample proposal $\mathbf{z}' \sim q(\mathbf{z}^{(n-1)})$; \\
    Calculate acceptance probability $\alpha(\mathbf{z}^{(n-1)}, \mathbf{z}')$ as in (\ref{eq:mhacceptance}); \\
    Sample $u \sim \text{Uniform}[0, 1]$ \\
    \eIf{$u < \alpha(\mathbf{z}^{(n-1)}, \mathbf{z}')$}{
    \text{Accept Proposal: }
    $\mathbf{z}^{(n)} \leftarrow \mathbf{z}'$;
    }{
    \text{Reject Proposal: }
    $\mathbf{z}^{(n)} \leftarrow \mathbf{z}^{(n-1)}$;
    }
}

\Return $\mathbf{Z} = \{\mathbf{z}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{z}^{(N)}\}$


 \caption{Metropolis-Hastings (MCMC) algorithm, adapted from \cite{Douc2019}.}
 \label{algo:mh}
\end{algorithm}

As such, MCMC methods are elegant yet practical algorithms to resolve the issues faced in Bayesian phylogenetic inference. The method itself also allows for a natural quantification of uncertainty through the discretisation of the posterior distribution. Furthermore, the method is highly flexible and can incorporate models and priors more complex than has been discussed in Section \ref{sec:bayesianphylomethods} so far.

However, MCMC methods suffer from several limitations. Given the Markov structure of the chain, proposals cannot be conducted and evaluated in parallel. The Markov structure also causes samples to be highly dependent, requiring a large number of iterations. These two limitations constrain MCMC methods to require a large number of samples, thus requiring much computational runtime. Additionally, inference may be sensitive to poor mixing and multimodality of the posterior distribution, which is a common issue considering the space of phylogenetic trees $\mathcal{T}$.

In particular, the algorithm does not guard against model misspecification issues. For example, if the likelihood function is misspecified through the violation of the conditional site independence critierion, the model does not address the potential biases arising from violations of the underlying modelling assumptions.



\subsection{Implementation in BEAST}
\label{sec:beast}
There are many packages available for practical implementations of MCMC methods on phylogenetic trees, one of which is the \textit{Bayesian Evolutionary Analysis Sampling Trees}, or BEAST algorithm \cite{beast_documentation}. Given the observed cognate data, BEAST samples from the posterior distribution and constructs a Markov chain as described in this section. In particular, tree space $\mathcal{T}$ is explored through a collection of local and global proposal operators to facilitate sufficient exploration. The package incorporates high performance computing methods \cite{beagle_documentation} outside the scope of this thesis that ensures an efficient practical implementation of the inference process.

In this thesis, BEAST is used as the inference engine for all inferences as detailed in Chapter \ref{sec:methods}. This enables a scalable and streamlined pipeline for direct assessment of the effect of model misspecification through conditional site dependence.

\section{Independent Paradigm}
\label{sec:independent}
Having discussed the entire methodology to recover phylogenetic trees, we examine the conditional site independence assumption in more detail. In particular, the effects of imposing the assumption in data generation, likelihood and posterior calculations and effective information brought forward by the (generated) data $\mathbf{D}$ will be considered. 

Under the \textit{independent paradigm}, that is, where the conditional site independence assumption is imposed, data generation for the simulation study is conducted in a straightforward manner as discussed in Section \ref{sec:ctmc}. In particular, Algorithm \ref{algo:ctmcdatageneration} is implemented to generate independent sites $\mathbf{D}_i$ for $i = 1, ..., S$, which ensures site independence throughout the data. 

We return to the likelihood calculations in (\ref{eq:sitelikelihood}). Clearly, on considering the full dataset $\mathbf{D}$, we can decompose the probability into a product of likelihood calculations at each site:
\begin{align}
    P(\mathbf{D} | T, \boldsymbol{\theta}) &= \prod_{i=1}^S P(\mathbf{D}_i | T, \boldsymbol{\theta}) \\
    &= \prod_{i=1}^S P(\mathbf{D}_i | T, \mathbf{G}) \label{eq:independentbreakdown}
    %&= \prod_{i=1}^S \left[\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_W \in \{0, 1\}^{|W|}} \pi_{x^{(i)}_r} \prod_{(u, v) \in E} \exp(\mathbf{G}t_{u\to v})_{x_u x_v}\right]
\end{align}
This reduces the likelihood calculations (and thus also the posterior calculations) related to the independent paradigm to the simpler problem of calculating likelihoods of individual sites, where we only require Felsenstein's pruning algorithm as described in Section \ref{sec:ctmc}. This implies that the likelihood calculation has a time-complexity of $\mathcal{O}(nS)$, scaling linearly with respect to number of languages $n$ and number of sites $S$.  

The independent paradigm also suggests that every new site provides an equal amount of information. From (\ref{eq:independentbreakdown} we can infer that the log-likelihood and thus the Fisher information of the model is additive with respect to the number of sites $S$. This means that an increase in $S$ should be inversely proportionate to the variance of the estimates obtained by the model. In the context of Bayesian phylogenetic analysis, more sites $S$ increases the model's ability to reconstruct the true tree $T^*$ and lead to better estimates.

\section{Dependent Paradigm}
\label{sec:dependent}
We turn our attention to the \textit{dependent paradigm}, where sites are not assumed to be generated independently from each other. The main implication of relaxing this assumption is that the decomposition in (\ref{eq:independentbreakdown}) is now invalid - we now have to consider how sites may interact with each other to determine the correct likelihood structure.

While there are many approaches to construct such dependencies within simulated data $\mathbf{D}$, we focus our discussion on a methodology such that sites are generated in a hierarchial format. We construct $L$ tiers of data, where data from Tier $1$ refers to independently generated sites, while data from Tier $l = 2, ..., L$ are sites that are dependent on certain sites from Tier $l-1$. A diagram of the dependent data generation methodology is described in Figure \ref{fig:dependentgeneration}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{report/figures/datageneration.png}
    \caption{A hierarchial approach to dependent site generation with $L$ tiers that generations data $\mathbf{D}$.}
    \label{fig:dependentgeneration}
\end{figure}

This method thus requires us to answer two design questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item How does each site in Tier $l$ \textit{choose} which site (which we will call the \textit{parent} site) in Tier $l-1$ to depend on? With reference to Figure 
    \ref{fig:dependentgeneration}, how are the start- and end-points arrows defining dependencies on previous tiers and sizes $N_2, \dots, N_L$ determined?
    \item Upon choosing a parent site from Tier $l-1$, how is each dependent site (which we call the corresponding \textit{child} site) in Tier $l$ generated given the parent site? With reference to Figure \ref{fig:dependentgeneration}, how do the arrows transmit dependencies from the site in the previous tier?
\end{enumerate}
The answers to these questions then define the manner in which data is generated, and thus how likelihoods will be calculated. For the purposes of this simulation study, we generate dependencies via the Zero-Inflated Poisson (ZIP), discussed in Section \ref{sec:zip}, and generate dependent site values by considering the product of the result of CTMC runs, discussed in Section \ref{sec:ctmcproduct}.

This method in particular is highly relevant to related works on dependent data. This hierarchial dependency structure corresponds to the issue posed by Maddison's red/blue tail problem \cite{Maddison1993}, where $L = 2$. Additionally, in the context of Bayesian phylolinguistic analysis, Buckley et al. (2025) considered loom data that also exhibited the same structure \cite{Buckley2025}, this time with $L = 4$. {\color{red} it might be worth saying that critics of the paper drew concerns about the model being misspecified on such data.} Consequently, investigating the extent of impact of applying Bayesian phylogenetic models, which assume conditional site independence, to hierarchially dependent data provides a frame of reference for assessing the consequences of model misspecification in settings where site dependencies are known or suspected to exist. Such analyses can thus address concerns of applying standard Bayesian phylogenetic models to data which exhibit such hierarchial dependency structures.

\subsection{ZIP Construction}
\label{sec:zip}
We start by specifying the \textit{Zero-Inflated Poisson} (ZIP) random variable which generates the hierarchial dependencies, which will in turn describe the sizes of each tier of data generated. 

\begin{definition} [Zero-Inflated Poisson (ZIP)]
    Let $X$ be a discrete random variable. $X$ is said to follow a Zero-Inflated Poisson with parameters $p \in [0, 1]$ and $\lambda \geq 0$, or $X \sim \text{ZIP}(p, \lambda)$ if its density is given by 
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:zip}
        f_X(x) = \begin{cases} p + (1-p)\exp(-\lambda) & x= 0 \\
        (1-p)\exp(-\lambda) \frac{\lambda^x}{x!} & x \neq 0
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    In other words, $X$ can be represented as a product of independent random variables $X = YZ$, where $Y \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1 - p), Z \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$.
\end{definition}

While dependency structure within linguistics data is widely acknowledged \cite{Longobardi2016}, the exact relationship between sites is not as clearly defined. The use of a ZIP random variable, instead of other conventional distributions like the standard Poisson distribution, is a design choice made with the context of historical linguistics in mind. The ZIP random variable is the generative model of choice as we require a flexible yet parsimonious model that allows both the absence of dependencies but also the substantial heterogeneity in number of dependent sites generated by a given site. By increasing $p$ (and thus increasing the value of $\lambda$ such that their expected values remain constant), the model generates dependencies much more \textit{sparsely}: for a given site, there are either no dependencies at all, or there are many dependencies. An example can be found in Figure \ref{fig:sparsegeneration}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/sparsegeneration.png}
    \caption{Sparse vs Dense Data Generation using the ZIP random variable, where boxes refer to sites. Note that the tier sizes used are for presentation purposes only: they need not be equal.}
    \label{fig:sparsegeneration}
\end{figure}

\begin{proposition}
    Let $X$ be a ZIP random variable with parameters $p$ and $\lambda$. Then:
    \begin{align}
        E(X) &= (1-p)\lambda =:\mu, \\
        Var(X) &= (1-p)\lambda + p(1-p)\lambda^2 = \mu(1+p\lambda) =:\sigma^2
    \end{align}
    \label{prop:zipevar}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    This result follows directly from decomposing the expectation and variance of a product of independent variables:
    \begin{align}
        E(X) &= E(YZ) \\
        &= E(Y)E(Z) \\
        &= (1-p)\lambda := \mu, \\
        Var(X) &= Var(YZ) \\
        &= E(Y^2Z^2) - (E(YZ))^2 \\
        &= E(Y^2)E(Z^2) - (E(Y)E(Z))^2 \\
        &= E(Y)E(Z^2) - (E(Y)E(Z))^2 \label{eq:squared} \\
        &= (1-p)(\lambda + \lambda^2) - (1-p)^2\lambda^2 \\
        &= (1-p)\lambda + p(1-p)\lambda^2 \\
        &= \mu(1 + p\lambda),
    \end{align}
    where we used $E(Y^2) = E(Y)$ of a Bernoulli distribution to derive (\ref{eq:squared}).
\end{proof}

This method of generating dependent data can also be seen as a branching process starting with $L_1$ individuals, with offspring distribution $\mathbf{d} = (P(X = k))_{k\geq 0}$, where $X \sim \text{ZIP}(p, \lambda)$. As such, we can interpret dependent tiers (Tiers $2, \dots, L)$ as later generations of the initial generation (Tier $1$), thus reframing the issue of determining the properties of random variables $N_2, ..., N_L$ in the context of finding the size of the population at a given generation. We can thus use results from branching processes to determine the site of the dataset at a given tier $l$:

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:zipgeneration}
    Suppose independent data is generated at tier $1$ with $N_1$ sites, where dependent data is generated via a branching process with offspring following a ZIP distribution. Then the site of tier $l$, given by $N_l$, is a random variable with mean and variance given as 
    \begin{align}
        E(N_l) &= N_1 \mu^{l-1}, \label{eq:branche}\\
        Var(N_l) &= \begin{cases}
            N_1 \sigma^2 \mu^{l-2} \frac{\mu^{l-1}-1}{\mu-1} & \mu \neq 1\\
            N_1 \sigma^2 (l-1) & \mu = 1
        \end{cases} \label{eq:branchvar},
    \end{align}
    where $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ is as defined in Proposition \ref{prop:zipevar}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    We employ theory from branching processes \cite{Dubrow2016}, where our initial population is $N_1$:
    \begin{align}
        E(N_l) &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty E(N_l|N_{l-1} = k) P(N_{l-1}=k) \\
        &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty E\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{l-1}} X^{(l-1)}_{i} | N_{l-1} = k\right) P(N_{l-1}=k) \\
        &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty E\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k} X^{(l-1)}_{i}\right) P(N_{l-1}=k) \\
        &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty k\mu P(N_{l-1}=k) \\
        &= \mu E(N_{l-1}),
    \end{align}
    where $X^{(l-1)}_{i} \sim \text{ZIP}(p, \lambda)$ representing the realisation of the offspring distribution for a single site $i$ in generation $l-1$. This results in a recursive relationship that gives (\ref{eq:branche}) as required. Similarly, for variance,
    \begin{align}
        Var(N_l) &= Var(E(N_l | N_{l-1}) + E(Var(N_l|N_{l-1}), \\
        E(N_l|N_{l-1} = k) &= \mu k, \\
        Var(N_l|N_{l-1} = k) &= Var\left(\sum_{i=1}^k X^{(l-1)}_{i}\right) = \sum_{i=1}^k Var\left(X^{(l-1)}_{i}\right) = \sigma^2 k, \\
        \implies Var(N_l) &=\mu^2 Var(N_{l-1}) + \sigma^2\mu^{l-2} \\
        &= \begin{cases}
            n\sigma^2 & \mu = 1\\
            \sigma^2 \mu^{l-2}\frac{\sigma^2 - 1}{\mu - 1}]& \mu \neq1 
        \end{cases}.
    \end{align}
\end{proof}

With these estimates of $N_l$, we are able to calculate the expected size of data given number of independent sites $N_1$, ZIP parameters $(p, \lambda)$ and number of tiers $L$. We also note that these calculations are performed before ascertainment as introduced in Section \ref{sec:ctmc}. As a result, we expect values of $N_l$ to be smaller due to the removal of $\mathbf{0}$ vector sites. 

\subsection{CTMC Product Construction}
\label{sec:ctmcproduct}
Next we turn to the second design question of Section \ref{sec:dependent} - determining the dependency approach. While there are many methods to create dependency, we do so by setting the dependent site as the product of two binary CTMCs, one of them being the parent site from the previous tier. More formally, for site $\mathbf{D}_{l, k}$ dependent on $\mathbf{D}_{l-1, j}$, we generate $\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l, k}$ via the same binary CTMC, and multiply the two vectors element-wise:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dependencystructure}
    \mathbf{D}_{l, k} := \mathbf{D}_{l-1, j} \odot \tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l, k},
\end{equation}
where we use $\odot$ as the element-wise product operator. This method was implemented for several reasons. Firstly, the method was chosen for its straightforward implementation in light of binary characters. Next, such a method which employs the same binary CTMC (with the same generator $\mathbf{G}$) reduces confounding factors, where any effects are caused by the dependency structure. Finally, sites will be present in a specific language if and only if both $(\mathbf{D}_{l-1, j})_i = (\mathbf{D}_{l, k})_i = 1$. This is consistent with Maddison's red/blue tail problem, where a given species would have to have a tail (set up as one site), as well as whether they have a blue or red tail \cite{Maddison1993}. 

We now prove the overall process induced from such a data generation method is a (non-binary) CTMC. The proofs as stated below will focus on the dependency from an independent site to site dependent on it. All other dependencies can be derived in a similar fashion.
\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:ctmcprod}
    Consider the data generation method in (\ref{eq:dependencystructure}) with generator $\mathbf{G} = \begin{pmatrix}
         - & q_{01} \\
         q_{10 } & - \end{pmatrix}$, 
         then $\mathbf{B}_{l, k}:= \{\mathbf{D}_{l-1, j}, \tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l, k}\}$ and is also a CTMC of states $\mathcal{S} := \{00, 01, 10, 11\}$, with generator
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ctmcprodgenerator}
    \mathbf{G}_{\text{prod}} = \begin{pmatrix}
        - & q_{01} & q_{01} & 0 \\
         q_{10} & - & 0 & q_{01} \\
         q_{10} & 0 & - & q_{01} \\
         0 & q_{10} & q_{10} & - \\
    \end{pmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    We consider the transition probabilities over time $\delta > 0$ through (\ref{eq:transitionmatrixevolution}) as a sum of matrices:
    \begin{align}
        \mathbf{P}(\delta) &= \exp(\mathbf{G}\delta) \\
        &= \mathbf{I} + \delta \mathbf{G} + o(\delta) \label{eq:expandP}
    \end{align}
    where we use $o(\delta)$ similarly to Definition \ref{def:birthprocess} but in a matrix form. With the constructed process $\mathbf{B}_{l, k}:= \{\mathbf{D}_{l-1, j}, \tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l, k}\}$, we consider the transition probabilities over time $\delta$ from state $x = (x_1, x_2)$ to $x' = (x_1', x_2')$:
    \begin{align}
        (\mathbf{P}_{\text{prod}}(\delta))_{x, x'} &= P(\mathbf{B}_{l, k, t + \delta} = x' | \mathbf{D}_{l, k, t} = x) \\
        &= P(\mathbf{D}_{l-1, j, t+\delta} = x_1', \tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l, k, t+\delta} = x_2' | \mathbf{D}_{l-1, j, t} = x_1, \tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l, k, t} = x_2) \\
        &= P(\mathbf{D}_{l-1, j, t+\delta} = x_1' | \mathbf{D}_{l-1, j, t} = x_1)P(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l, k, t+\delta} = x_2' | \tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l, k, t} = x_2) \\
        &= (\mathbf{P}(\delta))_{x_1, x_1'}(\mathbf{P}(\delta))_{x_2, x_2'}.
    \end{align}
    This result shows that we can represent the resulting transition probabilities as a Kronecker product $\mathbf{P}_{\text{prod}}(\delta) = \mathbf{P}(\delta) \otimes \mathbf{P}(\delta)$ \cite{Kronecker}. Using (\ref{eq:expandP}), we have that
    \begin{align}
        \mathbf{P}_{\text{prod}}(\delta) &= \mathbf{P}(\delta) \otimes \mathbf{P}(\delta) \\
        &= (\mathbf{I}_2  + \delta \mathbf{G} + o(\delta)) \otimes (\mathbf{I}_2 + \delta \mathbf{G} + o(\delta)) \\
        &= \mathbf{I}_2  \otimes \mathbf{I}_2  + \delta(\mathbf{G} \otimes \mathbf{I}_2 ) + \delta(\mathbf{I}_2  \otimes \mathbf{G}) + \delta^2(\mathbf{G} \otimes \mathbf{G}) + o(\delta) \\
        &=  \mathbf{I}_4 + \delta  (\mathbf{G} \otimes \mathbf{I}_2  + \mathbf{I}_2  \otimes \mathbf{G})+ o(\delta),
    \end{align}
    where $\mathbf{I}_n \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is the identity matrix in the $n$-dimensional square matrix to avoid confusion of notation. It is then clear that via a similar expansion to retrieve (\ref{eq:expandP}), $\mathbf{G}_{\text{prod}} = (\mathbf{G} \otimes \mathbf{I}_2  + \mathbf{I}_2  \otimes \mathbf{G})$. We prove that this gives (\ref{eq:ctmcprodgenerator}):
    \begin{align}
        \mathbf{G} \otimes \mathbf{I}_2  &= \begin{pmatrix}
            -q_{01}\mathbf{I}_2  & q_{01}\mathbf{I}_2  \\
            q_{10}\mathbf{I}_2  & -q_{10}\mathbf{I}_2 
        \end{pmatrix} \\
        &= \begin{pmatrix}
            -q_{01} & 0 & q_{01} & 0 \\
            0 & -q_{01} & 0 & q_{01} \\
            q_{10} & 0 & -q_{10} & 0 \\
            0 & q_{10} & 0 & -q_{10} \\
        \end{pmatrix}\label{eq:GxI}, \\
        \mathbf{I}_2 \otimes \mathbf{G} &= \begin{pmatrix}
            \mathbf{G} & \mathbf{0} \\
            \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{G} 
        \end{pmatrix} \\
        &= \begin{pmatrix}
            -q_{01} &  q_{01} & 0 & 0 \\
            q_{10} & -q_{10} & 0 & 0 \\
            0 & 0 & -q_{01} &  q_{01} \\
            0 & 0 & q_{10} & -q_{10} \\
        \end{pmatrix}\label{eq:IxG}.
    \end{align}
    The addition of (\ref{eq:GxI}) and (\ref{eq:IxG}) gives the result as required.
\end{proof}

A transition diagram of the resulting CTMC model can be found in Figure \ref{fig:ctmcprod}. We now derive the stationary distribution for such a model.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{report/figures/ctmcprod.png}
    \caption{Transition Diagram of Product of CTMCs $\mathbf{B}_{l, k}:= \{\mathbf{D}_{l-1, j}, \tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l, k}\}$. Blue states represent where $D_{l, k, i} = 0$, red state represents where $D_{l, k, i} = 1$, where $D_{l, k, i}$ represents the $i$-th element of $\mathbf{D}_{l, k, i}$.}
    \label{fig:ctmcprod}
\end{figure}

\begin{proposition}
    The unique stationary distribution of the resulting CTMC defined in Proposition \ref{prop:ctmcprod} with generator given in (\ref{eq:ctmcprodgenerator}) is
    \begin{align}
        \tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}} &= \boldsymbol{\pi} \otimes \boldsymbol{\pi} \\
        &= (\pi_0^2, \pi_0\pi_1, \pi_0\pi_1, \pi_1^2),
    \end{align}
    where $\boldsymbol{\pi} = (\pi_0, \pi_1)$ is the stationary distribution of the original CTMC defined in (\ref{eq:detailedbalance}).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    We use properties of a Kronecker product to propose $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}$ as a stationary distribution of the CTMC derived in Proposition \ref{prop:ctmcprod}. We already know from (\ref{eq:detailedbalance}), $\boldsymbol{\pi}\mathbf{G} = \mathbf{0}$, and thus
    \begin{align}
        (\boldsymbol{\pi} \otimes \boldsymbol{\pi}) (\mathbf{G} \otimes \mathbf{I}_2 ) &= \mathbf{0} \otimes \boldsymbol{\pi} = \mathbf{0}, \\
        (\boldsymbol{\pi} \otimes \boldsymbol{\pi}) (\mathbf{I}_2 \otimes \mathbf{G}) &=\boldsymbol{\pi} \otimes  \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}, \\
        \implies \tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\mathbf{G}_{\text{prod}} &= (\boldsymbol{\pi} \otimes \boldsymbol{\pi}) (\mathbf{G} \otimes \mathbf{I}_2 + \mathbf{I}_2 \otimes \mathbf{G}) \\
        &= (\boldsymbol{\pi} \otimes \boldsymbol{\pi}) (\mathbf{G} \otimes \mathbf{I}_2) + (\boldsymbol{\pi} \otimes \boldsymbol{\pi})(\mathbf{I}_2 \otimes \mathbf{G}) = \mathbf{0}.
    \end{align}
    Thus $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}$ is a stationary distribution of the CTMC with generator $\mathbf{G}_{\text{prod}}$. We now consider the uniqueness of $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}$. Clearly all states are connected, making the Markov chain generated from the CTMC irreducible. Since the state space is finite, we have that the CTMC must admit a unique stationary distribution \cite{Douc2019}.
\end{proof}

We note that when considering the dependent site $\mathbf{D}_{l, k}$, we compress the CTMC $\mathbf{B}_{l, k}$ as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{D}_{l, k} = \begin{cases}
        0 & \mathbf{B}_{l, k} \in \{00, 01, 10\} \\
        1 & \mathbf{B}_{l, k} \in \{11\}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

While this stochastic process is not a CTMC \footnote{\color{red}It does not satisfy the Markov property. This statement is justified in Appendix \ref{app:ctmcprodmarkov}.}, we note that the stationary distribution of the model does carry over as
\begin{equation}
    \breve{\boldsymbol{\pi}} = (1-\pi_1^2, \pi_1^2).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Likelihood Computation}
\label{sec:likelihoodcomp}
having now discussed the intricacies of the dependent data generation model, we turn to analyse the difficulties of analysing the likelihood computations of such a model. Clearly, conditional site independence does not hold anymore, and we are not able to decompose the likelihood in a feasible manner. This comes with the additional cost of having to account for the latent CTMC variables that contribute to each dependent data generation. 

Figure \ref{fig:dependentgeneration} provides a graphical interpretation, not just for understanding dependent data generation, but also of likelihood decomposition. This induces the dependency graph $\mathcal{G}$ (more precisely a directed acyclic graph), where edges (arrows) indicate how likelihoods of sites are dependent on another (parent) site. We present a high-level analysis that illustrates the complexity of likelihood computation under this model.

Given dependency graph $\mathcal{G}$. We can decompose the likelihood by separating independent sites, given by sets of sites $\mathcal{I}$ and $\mathcal{D}$ respectively:
\begin{align}
     P(\mathbf{D} | T, \boldsymbol{\theta}) &= \prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}P(\mathbf{D}_i|T, \boldsymbol{\theta})\prod_{j\in\mathcal{D}}P(\mathbf{D}_j|\mathcal{D}_{\text{pa}(j), T, \boldsymbol{\theta}}), \label{eq:dependentlikelihood} \\
     P(\mathbf{D}_j|\mathcal{D}_{\text{pa}(j), T, \boldsymbol{\theta}}) &= \sum_{\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l, k}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathbf{D_j} = D_{\text{pa}(j)} \odot \tilde{\mathbf{D}}_j\}}P\left(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{j}|T, \boldsymbol{\theta}\right),\label{eq:dependencyctmccalculation}
\end{align}

where $\mathbbm{1}$ is the indicator variable. The dependency structure thus induces statistical dependence between sites at both observed and parent nodes. This means that likelihood cannot be factorised site-wise, where exact likelihood evaluation requires considering the joint state of all sites within a dependency lineage. This increases the effective state space from a binary set of states to an exponentially increasing number of states, increasing the overall computational overhead of the calculation and thus the algorithm. Therefore, even with Felsenstein's pruning algorithm, the likelihood calculations make the dependency model infeasible for use.

\subsection{Model Misspecification}
\label{sec:misspecification}
As seen in Section \ref{sec:independent}, the likelihood calculations decompose into simple evaluations using Felsenstein's algorithm. On the other hand, as seen in Section \ref{sec:likelihoodcomp}, the time-complexity of the dependency structure causes any model that requires likelihood evaluation to be infeasible. As such, while applying the model that assumes conditional site independence causes the model to be misspecified on dependent data, the model is still able to generate reconstructions of trees within a reasonable amount of computational runtime.

However, there are several other reasons why a model assuming conditional site-independence may be preferred. Firstly, when fitting to real linguistic data, the dependency structure is usually unknown. This makes any meaningful application of model relaxing such an assumption require the user to manually specify the dependency structure $\mathcal{G}$, which often times requires many design choices and other assumptions about the data $\mathbf{D}$. On top of that, such a model requires a data generation algorithm as specified in this section thus far, which adds another design choice but also puts the model at risk of overparameterisation. In certain cases, where the size of data is small, this may cause identifiability issues and may be fundamentally impossible to apply since there are more parameters than sites. 

What remains to be seen, thus, is the effect of model misspecification under such models. While the assumption of conditional site independence yields substantial computational advantages, a model that is unable to recover the true tree $T^*$ with an acceptable level of accuracy has limited practical value. Additionally, as seen from (\ref{eq:dependencyctmccalculation}), if sites do exhibit dependency structure, marginal information obtained by including these sites is considerably lower than independent sites, thus decreasing the \textit{effective size} of the dataset obtained. Blindly applying an model with this independence assumption would then inflate confidence or introduce bias in estimates as the model assumes a larger amount of information can be gained from the data. In other words, understanding the extent of the effect on reconstruction accuracy and bias in using the simplified model assuming conditional site independence allows one to understand the trade-offs involved in linguistic tree reconstruction. 

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:evalmetrics}
We conclude this section with a discussion of evaluation metrics of both tree reconstruction and MCMC quality. The first section on tree recovery metrics (Section \ref{sec:treerecoverymetrics} considers methods for quantifying distances in the tree space $\mathcal{T}$, often dealing with a collection of trees $\tilde{T} := \{T_1, \dots, T_n\} \subseteq \mathcal{T}$ compared to some true tree $T^* \in \mathcal{T}$. The second section on MCMC diagnostics (Section \ref{sec:mcmcdiagnostics}) discusses the quantitative and qualitative methods to ensure the Markov chain is representative of the target posterior distribution $p^\star$ as detailed in Section \ref{sec:mcmc}.
\subsection{Tree Recovery Metrics}
\label{sec:treerecoverymetrics}
To evaluate and compare the effects of violating conditional site-independence in linguistic data on phylogenetic tree reconstruction, it is essential to quantify discrepancies between a set of inferred trees $\tilde{T} := \{T_1, \dots, T_n\} \subseteq \mathcal{T}$ to the true tree $T^*$. While the space of trees $\mathcal{T}$ is a non-Euclidean space, there have been many specialised methods derived to quantify distances between trees. 

The principal method for measuring distances between trees is the \textit{Robinson-Foulds} (RF) metric \cite{RF1981}. The method as defined in Definition \ref{def:rf} considers the clades formed in each tree and calculates the number of clades that only exist in one of the trees. The definition in (\ref{eq:rf}) is equivalent to $\text{RF}(T^*, T) = |\mathcal{C}(T^*)| + |\mathcal{C}(T)| - 2|\mathcal{C}(T^*) \cap \mathcal{C}(T)|$ \cite{Branco2024}. Clearly, a distance of zero between two trees with the same number of leaves implies that the trees have the same set of clades and are topologically equivalent, whereas larger RF distances will imply increasingly different tree structures.

\begin{definition} [Robinson-Foulds Distance]
\label{def:rf}
    The Robinson-Foulds (RF) Distance between two rooted trees $T$ and $T^*$, with unique labelling in leaves, is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:rf}
        \text{RF}(T^*, T) = |\mathcal{C}(T^*) \setminus \mathcal{C}(T)| \cup |\mathcal{C}(T) \setminus\mathcal{C}(T^*)|,
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathcal{C}(T) := \{C(v) | v \in V\}$ is the set of all clades in rooted tree $T = (V, E, r)$.
\end{definition}

The RF distance is highly interpretable and simple to compute, the method is widely used to compare phylogenetic trees. However, the metric treats all clade disagreements equally, wherever the are on the tree. This method thus fails to consider biological or linguistic significance of clades \cite{Branco2024}. For the purposes of the simulation study contained within this thesis, the RF distance is still used as it is standard across literature and is a computationally efficient algorithm, fitting the needs of scalable experiments.

Clearly, the RF distance has an upper bound (which is given as $2n-6$, for comparing trees of $n$ languages) \cite{Steel1993}. This allows us to compute the \textit{normalised RF} (nRF) distance, an extension of the RF distance \cite{Branco2024, Steel1993}, that normalises the RF-distance by its theoretical maximum, giving a distance score between $0$ and $1$ that allows comparison of pairs of trees with differing values of $n$. This is extremely useful in the context of the simulation study as detailed in Chapter \ref{sec:methods} as trees of varying sizes are compared.

In addition to the RF distance, we consider a separate clade recovery metric, the \textit{clade recovery rate}, that measures the proportion of internal clades of $T^*$ that are recovered in inferred tree $T$. This is analogous to the recall metric in supervised machine learning contexts and can be defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:claderecovery}
    \text{CR} = \frac{|\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}(T^*) \cap \mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}(T)|}{|\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}(T^*)|}
\end{equation}

Unlike RF distances, the method notably considers the set of all internal clades $\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}$ which thus explicitly evaluates the ability of the inference procedure to recover true groupings and thus explain distances in tree recovery more accurately. When considering a collection of trees $\tilde{T}$, one could then proportion the number of internal clades of $T^*$ that are \textit{highly} supported by $\tilde{T}$, that is, where the proportion of trees in $\tilde{T}$ that recover internal clade $c \in \mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}(T^*)$ exceeds a threshold like $0.9$.

Finally, it is worth considering how a collection of trees $\tilde{T}$ can be \textit{summarised} into a single tree for comparison. While these methods can be applied to all trees before considering the summary statistics of the values, another approach would be summarising the trees into some representative tree $\hat{T}$ before applying these methods. These methods are most common in Bayesian phylogenetic methods like MCMC where a posterior sample of trees are generated, rather than a single estimate \cite{OReilly2018, Tahiri2022, hoffmann_bayesian_2021}.

There are multiple techniques to define a representative tree, of which we will focus on two methods. Firstly, the majority-rule consensus (MRC) tree constructs a new tree $T$ that includes all clades $\mathcal{C}(
\mathcal{T}) := \bigcup_{T \in \mathcal{T}} \mathcal{C}(T)$ that are supported in more than 50\% of trees $\mathcal{T}$. This prioritises recovering as many of the highly supported clades, which may result in a non-binary tree instead. 

A separate approach instead is the maximum clade credibility (MCC) tree, which chooses a tree $T \in \tilde{T}$ which maximises clade credibility:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CC}(T) := \prod_{c \in \mathcal{C}(T)} P(c),
\end{equation}
where $P(c)$ denotes the posterior probability of clade $c$. This results in choosing a (binary) tree from the posterior sample that has the highest ``agreeability'' with the rest of the posterior. These contrasts in priorities allow both methods to reported frequently in Bayesian phylogenetic analyses to understand reconstruction accuracy \cite{OReilly2018}.

\subsection{MCMC Diagnostics}
\label{sec:mcmcdiagnostics}

We conclude this section by considering the methods to ensure quality of the Markov chain. If the chain is unable to reach stationarity, the samples from the chain would not represent the target distribution, the posterior, to an acceptable degree. As such, methods to ensure proper mixing are required to validate the results of any Bayesian inference algorithm.

We introduce two methods to ensure quality of a given Markov chain 
\begin{equation}
\left(\{\mathbf{z}^{(i)} = \{T^{(i)}, \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(i)}\}\}\right)_{i = 1, ..., N}.
\end{equation} 
Firstly, while slightly informal, it is often informative to consider the mixing behaviour of the MCMC chain visually, perhaps through a trace plot like in Figure \ref{fig:traceplot}. Poor mixing can arise due to a number of reasons such as ineffective hyperparameter tuning or high dimensionality of the parameter space. As such, visually verifying the mixing of a Markov chain allows one to identify trends, periods of poor exploration or failures to reach stationarity. 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{report/figures/traceplot.png}
    \caption{An example of a trace plot which has mixed well.}
    \label{fig:traceplot}
\end{figure}

In general, a well-mixed Markov chain should fluctuate around a region of the parameter space without exhibiting long-term trends. However, this is subjective to certain cases, where the posterior distribution is multi-modal for example, a well-mixed Markov chain would exhibit jumps between regions of the parameter space.

Next, we consider the autocorrelation sequence $(\rho_\tau)_{\tau \geq 1}$ of samples, which give rise to Effective Sample Size (ESS) as a measure for quality of samples:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ess}
    \text{ESS} = \frac{N}{1 + 2\sum_{\tau = 1}^\infty \rho_\tau}.
\end{equation}

The motivation for considering ESS is that the Markov chain generates dependent samples \cite{Gelman2013}. As such, when compared to an equally-sized independently generated data, the Markov chain holds less information. Through calculation of the ESS, we derive the size of independently generated samples from the posterior that would hold the same amount of information as the Markov chain. As such, a chain that has highly correlated samples will have a much smaller ESS. For the context of this study, we shall set $\text{ESS} \geq 200$ as a pragmatic threshold for the quality of samples obtained, with reference to the application \textit{Tracer} which sets the same benchmark for potentially problematic parameters \cite{Tracer}.

While (\ref{eq:ess}) is easily calculated for ordinary parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, the autocorrelation sequence is much harder to define for the tree space. Instead, applications such as BEAST report the ESS for numerical quantities such as log-posterior and log-likelihood \cite{beast_documentation}, which provide a Euclidean expression for the similarity between trees.

A common practice in Bayesian inference methods like MCMC is to perform \textit{thinning} of the Markov chain which retains only every $k$-th sample. While reducing the overall dataset size, the resulting chain has much lower autocorrelation values and thus might improve ESS and practically reduce computational overhead \cite{Gelman2013}. Of course, this still requires a large number of samples once stationarity has been achieved, which may require the user to increase the total number of samples anyway to maintain ESS values.
