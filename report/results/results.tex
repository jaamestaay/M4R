\chapter{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Having described the simulation study methods and validated implementation results in Chapter \ref{sec:methods}, we discuss the results obtained from Experiments 1 and 2, in an attempt to answer Questions 1 and 2 as introduced in Section \ref{sec:qns}. This chapter will be split into two sections, presenting the results from each experiment. An in-depth analysis of each experiment's results will be presented in Section \ref{sec:discussion}.

In a similar vein to Section \ref{sec:validation}, the metrics of concern are the normalised RF distances and clade recovery of trees to their true tree $T^*$. Additionally, we consider the performance of the model not only on the full set of posterior trees $\tilde{T}$ but also on the two aforementioned representative trees, namely the Consensus and MCC trees.

We note, however, as the factorial design covers analysis across a variety of parameters in the parameter space, we present relevant cases of value that portray the general trend observed throughout the study. We show the full results in Appendix \ref{app:fullresults}.

- Variance is not a key issue here: standard deviation intuitively decreases as the number of sites increases; we expect low data scenarios to be generally more uncertain on trees rather than in high data scenarios.
-- Throughout all plots of the figure we note the systematic depreciation of tree reconstruction performance of the model if the data generated has inherent dependency structures.
-- Clearly the limiting issue here is not the proportion of data that is dependent, but rather the amount of data. The largest gains across the dataset are not by fulfilling site independence throughout the data, but instead having more data, even if the data violates site-independence.

1e. Clade (MCC, Consensus)
- Generally same story portrayed. However, it is definitely interesting that the differences between proportions of independent-data are more pronounced in the MCC trees. As such the information determined from the data is much more important, of which there is less effective information when a higher proportion of data is site-dependent.
