\chapter{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Having described the simulation study methods and validated implementation results in Chapter \ref{sec:methods}, we discuss the results obtained from Experiments 1 and 2, in an attempt to answer Questions 1 and 2 as introduced in Section \ref{sec:qns}. This chapter will be split into two sections, presenting the results from each experiment. An in-depth analysis of each experiment's results will be presented in Section \ref{sec:discussion}.

In a similar vein to Section \ref{sec:validation}, the metrics of concern are the normalised RF distances and clade recovery of trees to their true tree $T^*$. Additionally, we consider the performance of the model not only on the full set of posterior trees $\tilde{T}$ but also on the two aforementioned representative trees, namely the Consensus and MCC trees.

We note, however, as the factorial design covers analysis across a variety of parameters in the parameter space, we present relevant cases of value that portray the general trend observed throughout the study. We show the full results in Appendix \ref{app:fullresults}.

\section{Experiment 1}

We first study changes in tree reconstruction quality of the posterior sample in Experiment 1. Figure \ref{fig:exp1nrfmainsmall} shows a clear distinction between the independent control data and any of the dependent data used for tree reconstruction: virtually any amount of data that violates site independence seems to \textit{contaminate} the tree reconstruction, with nRF values always increasing. On the other hand, however, the proportion of site-dependent data does not impact nRF values as much, varying minimally across the total number of tiers used. These differences in tree reconstruction quality are expected: having a higher proportion of data that satisfies conditional site independence reproduces psoterior samples that have smaller nRF values. Additionally, we note the increase in uncertainty shown in the 95\% confidence regions of the nRF. {\color{red} it could be that the model is able to then pick up on this dependency structure and account for it in uncertainty?} Figure \ref{fig:exp1clademainsmall} tells a similar story: data that violates conditional site independence is clearly unable to recover as many internal clades as in the control, yet proportion of site dependency does not contribute as much to this decrease.

Moreover, we see that the effects of violating conditional site independence on tree reconstruction are more dramatic where the total number of sites $S$ is low. In Figure \ref{fig:exp1nrfmainsmall}, the biggest increase in nRF is where there $S$ is low, increasing by 0.12-0.18, while the nRF barely changes where $S$ is large. In Figure \ref{fig:exp1clademainsmall}, where $S=50$, there is a roughly 6\% decrease in total clades recovered on average, whereas this drops to below 2\% where $S=5000$.

These figures show that there are indeed marginal losses in tree reconstruction as a result of presuming the independence assumption on data that violates it. However, the limiting issue here is not the proportion of data that is dependent, but rather the amount of information obtained from the data. The largest improvements of tree reconstruction as found in Figures \ref{fig:exp1nrfmainsmall} and \ref{fig:exp1clademainsmall} are not by fulfilling site independence throughout the data, but instead by having a larger amount of data to start with, even if the data violates conditional site independence. These findings corroborate with the validation plots found in Figures \ref{fig:val1nrf} and \ref{fig:val1clade}.

\section{Experiment 2}
We now examine whether adding data that has a known dependency structure to independent data is valuable to tree reconstruction. Figure \ref{fig:exp2nrfmainsmall} shows an excerpt of nRF values against total depth of dependency within the dataset. 

2a: RFs of Posterior

- Depending on the amount of independent data and the number of languages:

- \{tab\} If independent size is small and tree space is large, adding dependent data improves the model. However, if there exists sufficient data for the model to perform reconstruction better, then dependent data actually can hurt model performance. Similarly, adding data that has greater dependency depth follows the same relationship, but is more sensitive to these changes. This is best compared between the bottom left and top right subplots.

- Dense relationships in dependency structures are more favourable in general, with the blue line being the lowest across most comparisons. Additionally, the variance of posterior nRF means seem to follow closely with variance of data generation in Proposition \ref{prop:zipgeneration}: the amount of data generated in the sparse method varies the most and seems to have the largest 95\% confidence intervals, especially in low data scenarios.

2b: Clade Recovery of Posterior

- Largest benefit of including dependent data. Clade Recovery Rate of the model increases quite dramatically: up to 25\%. There is almost always an improvement to clade recovery. Including higher order tiers still seems to improve clade recovery, albeit at lower rates.

- Again, these improvements are more prounced where the original data size is low, which could signal that having more data does just improve the model's ability to reconstruct the tree, even though misspecification does decrease the benefit of doing so.

- Sparse generations perform more poorly than dense generations, as seen in the nRF case. 

- Variance of posterior clade recovery scales with sparsity as seen in the nRF case as well.
