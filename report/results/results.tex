\chapter{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Having described the simulation study methods and validated implementation results in Chapter \ref{sec:methods}, we discuss the results obtained from Experiments 1 and 2, in an attempt to answer Questions 1 and 2 as introduced in Section \ref{sec:qns}. This chapter will be split into two sections, presenting the results from each experiment. An in-depth analysis of each experiment's results will be presented in Section \ref{sec:discussion}.

In a similar vein to Section \ref{sec:validation}, the metrics of concern are the normalised RF distances and clade recovery of trees to their true tree $T^*$. Additionally, we consider the performance of the model not only on the full set of posterior trees $\tilde{T}$ but also on the two aforementioned representative trees, namely the Consensus and MCC trees.

We note, however, as the factorial design covers analysis across a variety of parameters in the parameter space, we present relevant cases of value that portray the general trend observed throughout the study. We show the full results in Appendix \ref{app:fullresults}.

\section{Experiment 1}
We first study changes in nRF of the posterior sample in Experiment 1 which can be found in Figure \ref{fig:exp1nrfmainsmall}. We can note a clear distinction between the independent control data and any of the dependent data used for tree reconstruction: virtually any amount of data that violates site independence seems to \textit{contaminate} the reconstruction, with nRF values always increasing. On the other hand, however, the proportion of site-dependent data does not impact nRF values as much, varying minimally across the total number of tiers used. These differences in tree reconstruction quality are expected: having a higher proportion of data that satisfies conditional site independence reproduces psoterior samples that have smaller nRF values. Additionally, we note the increase in uncertainty shown in the 95\% confidence regions of the nRF. {\color{red} it could be that the model is able to then pick up on this dependency structure and account for it in uncertainty?} Figure \ref{fig:exp1clademainsmall} tells a similar story: data that violates conditional site independence is clearly unable to recover as many internal clades as in the control, yet proportion of site dependency does not contribute as much to this decrease.

Moreover, we see that the effects of violating conditional site independence on tree reconstruction are more dramatic where the total number of sites $S$ is low. In Figure \ref{fig:exp1nrfmainsmall}, the biggest increase in nRF is where there $S$ is low, increasing by 0.12-0.18, while the nRF barely changes where $S$ is large. In Figure \ref{fig:exp1clademainsmall}, where $S=50$, there is a roughly 6\% decrease in total clades recovered on average, whereas this drops to below 2\% where $S=5000$.

These figures show that there are indeed marginal losses in tree reconstruction as a result of presuming the independence assumption on data that violates it. However, the limiting issue here is not the proportion of data that is dependent, but rather the amount of information obtained from the data. The largest improvements of tree reconstruction as found in Figures \ref{fig:exp1nrfmainsmall} and \ref{fig:exp1clademainsmall} are not by fulfilling site independence throughout the data, but instead by having a larger amount of data to start with, even if the data violates conditional site independence. These findings corroborate with the validation plots found in Figures \ref{fig:val1nrf} and \ref{fig:val1clade}.

We turn to the analyses of the MCC and Consensus trees.


1d. nRF (MCC, Consensus)
- It seems that the consensus tree actually performs the best out of all of them. But that could be because consensus trees are allowed to multifurcate where exact clade structures of the model are uncertain. RF typically rewards making conservative claims, which thus allows the nRF to perform much better than the posterior trees or MCC tree which are forced to make decisions on bifurcating structure for a given true tree $T^*$. Otherwise, no other differences.

1e. Clade (MCC, Consensus)
- Generally same story portrayed. However, it is definitely interesting that the differences between proportions of independent-data are more pronounced in the MCC trees. Perhaps this is the result of having to pick a specific `best' tree amongst the posterior sample, which cannot benefit from averaging across multiple trees. As such the information determined from the data is much more important, of which there is less effective information when a higher proportion of data is site-dependent.
